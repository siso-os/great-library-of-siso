C-0002 — Prior art: who already solved this?

STATUS: drafted  ·  BUDGET: one deep-research lane  ·  Uses: deep research, not code execution

QUESTION

Cross-domain person resolution with reversible decisions, evidence receipts and rights propagation is not a new problem — it is authority control, a library-science discipline with roughly a century of institutional learning. Who has already solved it at scale, what did they learn the hard way, and which of our 100 tracks does that make redundant?

WHY FRONTIER

  • Highest ROI per token on the register. The plausible outcome is deleting work, not adding it. A track we do not have to build is worth more than one we build well.
  • Wrong is expensive in a quiet way: re-deriving a solved discipline costs months and nobody notices, because the work looks productive throughout.
  • Durable: VIAF's merge/split semantics and ORCID's disambiguation failures will still be true in five years.
  • Needs genuine synthesis across library science, scholarly infrastructure and software provenance — three literatures that rarely cite each other. That is the discriminating skill.

WHAT TO INVESTIGATE

  1. VIAF — clusters merge and split as national libraries disagree. How is that reconciled? What breaks? Their freshness problems are already flagged as a gate in our source matrix.
  2. ORCID — self-asserted identity at scale. What fraction of records are duplicates, and what did they learn about letting people claim their own identifiers?
  3. Wikidata — reversible, evidenced, community-adjudicated claims with deprecation ranks. Closest existing analogue to our assertion model. What are its known failure modes?
  4. Library authority files (LoC/NACO, ISNI) — a century of rules for "same person?" including pseudonyms, corporate bodies and undifferentiated names.
  5. Software Heritage — durable artifact identity across renames, transfers and takedowns. Directly relevant to our GitHub rename problem.
  6. Scholarly disambiguation literature — measured precision/recall for author-name disambiguation. What accuracy is actually achievable, and at what review cost? This calibrates whether GQ-010's ≥99.5% target is realistic or fantasy.

FALSIFIERS

  1. The prior art turns out not to transfer — their populations, incentives or funding models differ so fundamentally that the lessons do not apply. Say so plainly; that is a real result.
  2. No institution reports measured precision, in which case GQ-010's 99.5% target has no external calibration and should be restated as an assumption rather than a criterion.
  3. The recommendation is "build it ourselves anyway" — acceptable only with a specific reason per rejected option.

DELIVERABLE

  • A synthesis with citations to primary sources, not summaries of summaries.
  • A verdict per institution: adopt the model / adopt the vocabulary / learn from the failure / not applicable, each with its reason.
  • The list of the 100 tracks this makes redundant, with the prior art that supersedes each. This is the primary deliverable — everything else is supporting.
  • An external calibration for achievable identity precision and its review cost.
  • Lands as a research document in the Great Library. Draft PR only.

NOT IN SCOPE

  • Re-running the OSS tooling evaluation (done, evidenced).
  • Retrying the GitHub↔book stitch. Measured dead, ceiling 419, zero plausible matches.
  • Ingesting anything. Research only.
  • Recommending a vendor or platform migration.
